# A Tale of Foolish Brilliance

I would like to begin this blog with a tale about [Ronald Fisher (1890 – 1962)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher). For those unaware, Fisher is arguably one of the greatest statisticians of all time. His work largely pioneered the foundations of statistical science. Concepts such as null hypotheses, maximum likelihood estimation, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and $p$-values were either created or formalized by him. Suffice it to say, Fisher was a genius who singlehandedly transformed entire fields.

However, Fisher was an incredibly controversial figure. As a person, he was brash, hotheaded, and unpleasant. As an academic, he was a staunch eugenicist. Most controversial during his time was his disputes with public health officials over smoking. He vehemently denied that smoking caused health problems, dismissing such claims as "[terrorist propaganda](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2022/06/08/how-can-a-top-scientist-be-so-confidently-wrong-r-a-fisher-and-smoking-example/)", despite mounting evidence to the contrary.

None of this stupidity is unique to Fisher. Lord Kelvin, the physicist namesake of the temperature scale, believed X-ray and radio were hoaxes. Thomas Edison, a prolific inventor, believed women were incapable of direct thought. George Patton, a skilled general during the Second World War, believed shell shock was a myth.

Every listed individual is undoubtedly talented in their craft, to the extent that their ideas are still studied today. Nevertheless, it is difficult to fathom that a single person is simultaneously capable of such stupidity and such brilliance.

# A Simple Explanation

While I lack the qualifications to discuss the merits behind theories of intelligence, I believe mentioning them better motivates my personal beliefs. Of course, one should not take this blog as authoritative science.

A common explanation for competency in one skill and incompetency in others is the [theory of multiple intelligences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_multiple_intelligences) (MI). In its original proposal, MI argued that intelligence can be divided into the categories musical, visual-spatial, linguistic, logical-mathematical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, and intrapersonal. In this way, intelligence represents multiple distinct traits rather than a single overarching trait.

Under MI, it is immediate why a person may be simultaneously brilliant and idiotic. In the case of George Patton, his wartime genius can be attributed to talent in logical-mathematical skills, while his denial of shell shock can be attributed to deficiencies in interpersonal skills{{ footnote() }}.

However, MI is highly contentious, with modern research suggesting the existence of general intelligence alongside specialized intelligences. Moreover, it fails to explain a case like Fisher. On one hand, his work in statistics demonstrate exceptional logical-mathematical skills. On the other hand, his criticism against smoking findings were largely fallacies of imprecise defenses or cherry-picked counterexamples, suggesting gaps in logical-mathematical skills.

# The Case of Fisher

When reading his personal history, there is a fairly straightforward explanation for why Fisher opposed public health officials so strongly. In addition to a disagreeable personality, he was a smoker himself. From his perspective, a new generation of scientists are arguing against *smoking* with *statistics*, an insult to two of his identities. After all, Fisher spent most of his career developing statistical methodology, so it must be frustrating to view what he believes to be misconduct, particularly with something so close to heart.

There's certainly more nuance, but the underlying theme here is that pride clouded his judgment. A theme common among tales of brilliance. To quote Mahatma Gandhi on a related matter.

> It is unwise to be too sure of one's own wisdom. It is healthy to be reminded that the strongest might weaken and the wisest might err.

I resonate with this quote deeply, and from it, I have a personal explanation for intelligence coexisting with stupidity.

# A Personal Explanation

Intelligence is commonly seen as a characteristic of individuals. After all, we remember people like Albert Einstein as geniuses of unmatched caliber. But rather than a simple trait, I believe brilliance represents an upper bound on one's capabilities. In this way, intellect fundamentally represents a maximal limit before a tendency.

"Upper bound on one's capabilities" is pointing in the right direction, but still much too vague. The phrase hints at the necessity for skepticism of one's ideas and a degree of humility. Developing significant results may suggest that future work will be just as impactful, yet it's important to distinguish tendency from certainty. For the case of Fisher, it's undeniable that he possessed superb skill with statistical inference, and would continue to publish fine ideas if he were alive today. Nevertheless, as his smoking debacle demonstrates, he is still prone to fallacious and poor assessments.

Alas, the premise of my explanation reveals many unaddressed questions. If we have an upper bound, can we find a lower bound? Rather than bounding competency, can we describe its behavior? Asked differently, why do some people tend to act more brilliantly than more stupidly, or vice versa?

To the first question on lower bounds, I believe there is none. All humans are capable of grand stupidity, irrespective of how intelligent they may be. I think this is partly what it means to be human.

To the second, however, I lack an explanation. My hunch tells me that guiding tendencies is the role of experience, confidence, and aptitude, with arrogance, stubbornness, etc. serving as obstacles. Nonetheless, I believe offering a proper explanation requires a level of expertise beyond informal ideas. Moreover, it detracts from the central theme of this blog, which is the importance of skepticism with oneself. Else you become the subject of a PhD student's blog on the duality of stupidity and intelligence.
